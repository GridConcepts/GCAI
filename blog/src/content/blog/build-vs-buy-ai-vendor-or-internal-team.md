Somewhere around the second or third AI use case, most COOs get the same piece of advice from inside the business: "why are we paying a vendor for this - couldn't we just hire someone?" It's a fair question, and build vs. buy is the right decision to make deliberately rather than drift into. But the comparison only works if both sides are costed honestly. A data scientist's salary is the easy number to find. What it takes to turn one hire into a working, maintained system is usually the number nobody has actually priced.

## What "build" really costs

The headline cost is the salary, and in Australia that's genuinely competitive: SEEK data puts data scientist salaries at roughly $115,000 to $135,000, with senior machine-learning specialists in Sydney and Melbourne reaching $180,000 to $215,000 or more. Add superannuation, tooling, cloud compute and recruitment fees, and one experienced hire easily clears $200,000 a year fully loaded - before they've shipped anything.

That's the visible part. The parts that don't show up on the offer letter are what usually break the build case for a single-site SME:

- **One person is not a team.** A single data scientist can prototype a model. Taking it into production - integrating it with your CRM or practice-management system, monitoring it, fixing it when it breaks on a Friday afternoon - needs a second skill set (engineering) that the same person rarely has. You're often hiring for two roles and budgeting for one.
- **Maintenance doesn't stop at launch.** Industry estimates put ongoing upkeep - monitoring, retraining, validation - at roughly 15% to 25% of the original build cost every year, for as long as the system runs. That's a recurring line item most build business cases never model past year one.
- **The talent market is working against you.** The Australian Computer Society's Digital Pulse 2026 report found the domestic tech workforce actually shrank in 2025 even as the economy grew, with the country needing an estimated 259,000 additional technology professionals by 2035. A good AI hire in Melbourne is genuinely hard to find and harder to keep - and when they leave, the system they built leaves with their knowledge of how it works.

None of this means building is wrong. It means the honest cost of build is "salary plus a second hire plus 15-25% a year forever," not just the number on the offer letter.

## What "buy" really costs

Engaging an AI vendor trades that fixed headcount cost for a different set of risks, and they're worth naming just as plainly:

- **Usage-based pricing can scale faster than you expect.** A tool priced cheaply during a pilot can get materially more expensive once it's handling full production volume - read the pricing model past the pilot tier before you commit, not after.
- **Lock-in is real if the contract doesn't address it.** If a vendor owns the integration, the data model and the only documentation of how it works, switching later gets expensive. The fix is contractual, not something you can bolt on afterwards: ask upfront who owns the data, the integration code and the right to export everything cleanly.
- **Not every vendor is accountable for an outcome.** Some sell a tool and leave the implementation to you; others are structured to own the result. That distinction matters more than the price on the quote, and it's worth confirming before you sign anything.

The honest cost of buy is "vendor fee plus the work of managing that relationship well," and a vendor relationship managed badly can be just as expensive as an in-house team managed badly.

## The build vs. buy question that actually decides it

Skip "which is cheaper" - on a fair accounting, they're often closer than either side's pitch suggests. The question that actually separates the two cases is: **does this capability create an advantage specific to our business, or is it something every business in our position needs in roughly the same form?**

- If the answer is the latter - most quoting, scheduling, CRM and document-automation use cases fall here - buying from a vendor who's already solved it for businesses like yours is almost always faster and cheaper than re-solving it internally.
- If the answer is the former - a model trained on data or a process genuinely unique to how you operate, that a competitor couldn't replicate by buying the same tool - building (or co-building with a partner) starts to make sense, because the advantage is the point.

Gartner has forecast that more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be abandoned by the end of 2027 due to escalating costs, unclear business value, and inadequate risk controls - and that failure mode hits build and buy decisions equally when the underlying question was skipped. The businesses getting this wrong aren't choosing the wrong side of the ledger; they're choosing a side before asking whether the capability was ever worth owning in the first place.

## A simpler test for a resource-constrained SME

If you don't have the budget to build a proper team (engineering and data science both, not just one hire), buying isn't a compromise - it's the realistic option, and you can revisit build later once a use case has proven its value and its advantage is clear enough to justify owning it outright. Most Victorian SMEs are better served starting there: prove the use case with a vendor or implementation partner, keep the contract terms clean on data ownership and exit, and only consider building in-house once you can name the specific advantage that justifies the cost of owning it.
