Most AI agencies will show you an impressive demo. Fewer will survive the questions that come after it. Before you hire an AI agency, it pays to ask a short list of practical questions — because the research on why AI projects fail almost never points at the technology. It points at who was hired, and what was agreed before the work started.

RAND Corporation puts the failure rate for AI projects at more than 80%, roughly double the rate for ordinary IT projects. MIT's Project NANDA found that around 95% of generative AI pilots show no measurable return on the profit-and-loss statement. Gartner's own research is blunter still: 73% of failed AI projects had no agreed definition of success before they began, while projects that did define success upfront succeeded 54% of the time against just 12% for those that didn't. None of that is a technology problem. It's a selection and scoping problem — and it's fixable before you sign anything.

## The questions to ask before you hire an AI agency

Ask these in the first conversation, not after the contract is signed.

1. **What exactly will this automate first, and how will I know it worked?** A named metric, a before-and-after number — not "you'll see efficiency gains." If they can't point to one workflow (quoting, scheduling, after-hours enquiries, whatever it is for you) and one number that should move, they haven't scoped the job yet.
2. **Who owns this once you're gone?** Every AI project we've seen actually stick has one named person on the client side accountable for it. If the agency's answer is vague about who on your team runs it day to day, that's your answer.
3. **What does it cost, all in?** Not just the build — the ongoing cost of running it, the cost if your systems need cleaning up first, and what happens if the scope grows.
4. **Where does my data go, and who else can see it?** Under the Privacy Act, your obligations under the Australian Privacy Principles don't disappear because a vendor is now holding the data. A credible agency should be able to tell you, without checking, which systems your customer information touches, where it's stored, and whether it's used to train anything beyond your own account.
5. **Can you show me this working on my data, live, in ten minutes?** Not a generic demo — your actual quoting sheet, your actual CRM export. An agency that hesitates here is selling a capability, not a fit.
6. **What happens if it doesn't work?** A clear off-ramp, not a locked-in retainer, is a sign they expect to be judged on results.

## If it's framed as a pilot, ask two more

If the engagement is being pitched as a pilot rather than a full rollout — which it should be, for a first project — add:

- **What are the defined success criteria, and who signs off that they were met?** A pilot without agreed criteria is just a longer, more expensive demo.
- **What's the path from pilot to production if it works?** If the agency can't describe what happens after a successful pilot, the pilot is the whole plan — and that's how pilots quietly die.

## The honest-broker test

The single best signal is what the agency says about your own readiness. One that walks straight past questions about your data and systems into a proposal is optimising for the sale. One that says "here's what needs sorting first, and here's roughly what that costs before we touch AI" is optimising for a project that survives contact with your business. That second answer costs them a slower sales cycle. It's also the one worth paying for.
