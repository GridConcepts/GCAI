The one idea to take away

2026 is the year AI stops being a chatbot you talk to and starts being a digital worker that does things — books jobs, chases invoices, updates records. That's a bigger jump than it sounds, and the businesses that get value from it aren't the ones with the flashiest tool. They're the ones with tidy data, connected systems, clear rules, and a team that's been brought along for the ride. That's what the rest of this report walks you through.

Four things worth knowing before you spend another dollar on AI

  • 1
    The gap is a foundations gap, not an ambition gap. Victorian small businesses aren't short on interest — nearly a third rank AI as a top growth enabler. What's missing is tidy data, connected tools, and a bit of structure underneath.
  • 2
    Your other apps are already doing this for you. You probably don't need to "build" AI. Your accounting, CRM or booking software likely already has AI features quietly switched on or one setting away.
  • 3
    Fast, cheap AI beats slow, expensive AI for most small business tasks. You don't need the most powerful (and priciest) AI model for routine jobs like drafting a reply or summarising notes — save the expensive tools for genuinely hard problems.
  • 4
    Trust is now a business asset. 83% of Australians want to be told when a business is using AI on their information. Being upfront about it is a selling point, not a risk to hide.
Background

A very short history of AI (so the jargon makes sense)

You don't need a computer science degree to run a small business with AI in it. But five minutes of history explains why everyone's suddenly talking about "agents" in 2026.

1950s — The idea is born

Mathematician Alan Turing asks "can machines think?" A group of researchers at Dartmouth College coins the term "Artificial Intelligence" in 1956. It's pure theory — computers can barely add up.

1960s–1980s — Big promises, "AI winters"

Early programs can play simple games and follow rigid rules, but can't cope with the real world. Funding and hype rise and collapse more than once when the technology can't keep its promises — a pattern worth remembering today.

1997 — AI proves it can beat us at narrow tasks

IBM's Deep Blue beats the world chess champion. A reminder: AI has always been better at narrow, well-defined tasks than open-ended ones — still true today.

2010s — AI quietly moves into daily life

Voice assistants, spam filters, Netflix recommendations and Google Translate all run on AI under the hood. Nobody calls it "using AI" — it's just software working better.

November 2022 — The "magic" moment

ChatGPT launches to the public and AI chat goes mainstream overnight. For the first time, anyone can have a conversation with a computer that feels genuinely useful. This kicks off two years of experimentation — the "chat era."

2023–2025 — Everyone experiments, few scale

Businesses everywhere trial AI chatbots for emails, marketing copy and customer questions. Useful, but limited: a chatbot can only answer what you ask it — it can't go and do something on its own.

2026 — The "muscle" era: AI that acts, not just chats

The technology shifts from AI you talk to, to AI "agents" that can carry out multi-step tasks on your behalf — checking stock, updating a booking, chasing an overdue invoice — with a person still setting the rules and checking the work. This is the shift this report is mostly about.

02 · The Big Shift

From AI Chat to AI Agents

This is the single biggest change in 2026, and the one most likely to affect your business whether you plan for it or not.

💬

AI Chat (what you've probably used)

Think of it as a very well-read assistant you talk to. You ask a question or give it a task in words, and it writes an answer, a draft, or a summary. It's helpful — but it stops there. You still have to go and do the actual work yourself: copy the draft into an email, log into your booking system, update the spreadsheet.

⚙️

AI Agents (what's arriving now)

An agent doesn't just answer — it acts. Give it a goal ("chase this overdue invoice" or "book this customer's next service") and, within limits you set, it checks the right systems, fills in the right forms, and gets the job actually done. Think digital team member, not digital typewriter.

"If generative AI gave computers the ability to create, agentic AI gives them the ability to decide and act."

Samuel Irvine Casey, Partner, Data & AI, Mantel

Three flavours of "AI agent" — and which one is realistic for a small business

Start here

The Helper, built into tools you already own

An invisible feature inside software you already use — your CRM, accounting package, or booking system — quietly doing small jobs like ranking your best leads or drafting a follow-up email. You don't "build" anything; you switch it on.

Real example (enterprise scale, same idea applies smaller): an agent inside a CRM that automatically ranks incoming leads and drafts a personalised follow-up. — Mantel case study

Medium effort

The Assistant, a conversation that gets things done

You describe what you want in plain language, and the agent handles the multi-step process — gathering documents, checking details, preparing paperwork — then shows you its work before anything is finalised. Needs more setup and more careful checking, because it's doing more.

Real example: a home-loan assistant that gathers documents, runs credit checks and prepares the application, rather than just describing loan options. — Mantel case study, Lendi

Enterprise-grade, not for most SMEs yet

The Autonomous Worker, runs unsupervised

A fully independent "digital employee" that watches for triggers and acts without a human approving each step — for example, automatically adjusting rosters when a weather alert hits. Powerful, but expensive to build and maintain safely, and needs strong guardrails ("circuit breakers") to stop it going wrong. Most small businesses should watch this space rather than build it themselves.

What this means for you

  • Start by checking the tools you already pay for — Xero, MYOB, your CRM, your booking platform — many already have "Helper"-style AI features you haven't switched on.
  • Don't chase the flashiest "autonomous agent" demo you see online. Match the tool to the size of the job.
  • Keep a human checking the output until you trust the pattern — that's not being behind the times, it's how every business doing this well operates in 2026.
03 · Reality Check

The main reasons AI projects in small businesses fail

The technology almost never causes the failure. Research on business AI projects — small and large — points to the same handful of avoidable mistakes, over and over.

95%
of AI pilot projects deliver no measurable business benefit, based on a study of 300 real deployments and 350 employees.
MIT NANDA, "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business," 2025
2 in 3
projects that buy a ready-made AI tool from a specialist succeed — roughly triple the success rate of businesses trying to build their own AI in-house from scratch.
MIT NANDA, 2025

Five avoidable mistakes

  • 1
    Starting with the tool, not the problem. "We should use AI for something" is backwards. Start with a task that already costs you real hours each week, then ask if AI can take it on.
  • 2
    Feeding it messy information. An AI tool is like a new employee who only knows what you've told it. If your customer or stock records are scattered across spreadsheets, sticky notes and someone's memory, the AI inherits that mess.
  • 3
    Nobody owns it after launch. A tool gets switched on with excitement, nobody is responsible for checking it's still accurate a few months later, and it quietly gets abandoned or ignored.
  • 4
    Building instead of buying. Trying to build a custom AI system from scratch is expensive, slow, and roughly three times more likely to fail than adopting an established, proven tool built for your industry.
  • 5
    Skipping the people. Staff are handed a new tool with no training and no explanation of why — so they quietly avoid it, misuse it, or use it in ways that create risk. More in the People section below.

Rule of thumb

Fix the plumbing before you install the shiny tap. Every pitfall above is really the same root cause wearing a different hat: skipping the unglamorous groundwork — good data, clear ownership, and a trained team — in favour of jumping straight to the tool.

04 · Foundations, Part 1

The importance of reliable data behind AI

"Data" sounds technical. In a small business it just means: your customer list, your stock levels, your invoices, your bookings — wherever they currently live.

"Think of it like a Subway sandwich shop: you need your ingredients prepped, sliced, and organised in the display before the lunch rush."

Thomas Maas, Head of Client Solutions, Mantel — on preparing data before deploying AI

AI tools don't "understand" your business the way a long-serving staff member does. They only know what's in front of them. If your records are out of date, duplicated, or split across five different spreadsheets and apps that don't talk to each other, the AI won't magically see through the mess — it will confidently give you an answer built on bad information. That's arguably worse than no answer at all, because it sounds right.

76%
of Australian data & analytics leaders feel pressure to roll out AI quickly.
itbrief.com.au, ANZ data leaders survey, 2025
41%
of those same leaders don't fully trust their AI outputs — because the underlying data is outdated or sitting in disconnected systems.
itbrief.com.au, ANZ data leaders survey, 2025

A simple three-question data check-up

QuestionWhy it matters
Where does our customer/stock/booking information actually live?If the honest answer is "in three different places, plus Dave's inbox," an AI tool can only ever see part of the picture.
Is it current, or is it "mostly right"?AI trained on stale data will confidently produce stale, wrong answers — with no warning label attached.
Who's responsible for keeping it clean?If the answer is "nobody in particular," that's the first thing to fix — before adding any AI tool on top.

What this means for you

  • Pick one "single source of truth" for your core records (customers, stock, jobs) rather than letting the same information live in five places.
  • Spend a day cleaning up before you scale up — it's the least exciting hour of any AI project and the one that determines whether the rest works.
  • Give one person (even part-time) clear responsibility for data quality. It doesn't need a fancy title — it needs a name attached to it.
05 · Foundations, Part 2

Are your systems integrated?

"Integration" is a technical word for a very ordinary question: do your apps talk to each other, or does a person have to be the messenger between them?

Picture a typical small business: bookings come in through one app, invoices go out through accounting software, and customer notes live in a CRM or a notebook. If someone has to manually re-type information from one system into another, that's a sign your systems aren't integrated — and it means an AI agent can't safely act on your behalf, because it can't see the full picture either. It would be like asking a new staff member to do their job with one hand tied behind their back.

In plain English

Modern software increasingly has a shared "language" that lets different apps pass information between them safely and automatically — this is what people mean by systems being "integrated" or having "an API." When your accounting software, CRM and booking system are connected this way, an AI tool can be given a task and actually carry it out end-to-end. When they're not connected, a human still has to be the go-between, no matter how clever the AI is.

A quick integration audit

Fragmented (most common starting point)Integrated (what you're aiming for)
Staff re-type the same customer details into two or three different systemsOne update in any system flows through to the others automatically
"Let me just check our other system and get back to you"Front-of-house staff (or an AI helper) can see the full customer picture in one place
Spreadsheets exported and emailed between team membersShared, live data that everyone — and any AI tool — reads from the same source

What this means for you

  • Before buying a new AI tool, check whether your existing core systems (accounting, CRM, bookings, point-of-sale) already connect to each other — most mainstream small-business platforms now do this natively, it's often just a setting to switch on.
  • Prioritise connecting the two or three systems your team touches every single day, rather than trying to connect everything at once.
  • If a vendor tells you their AI feature "just works" with your other tools, ask them to show you — don't take it on faith.
06 · Trust & Safety

What about governance & security?

"Governance" just means having clear rules for how AI is used in your business — what it can see, what it can't, and who checks its work. You don't need a 40-page policy document; you need a few sensible rules everyone actually follows.

Three real risks, explained simply

Risk 1

Accidentally leaking information

Someone pastes a customer list or financial details into a public AI chat tool to "get a quick answer" — and that information may now sit outside your business's control.

Risk 2

Confidently wrong answers

AI tools can state incorrect information with total confidence ("hallucinating"). Fine for a first draft; risky if it goes straight to a customer or a compliance document unchecked.

Risk 3

Trusting the wrong supplier

Every AI tool you use is really a decision to trust a third-party company with some of your data. Cheap or unknown tools deserve the same scrutiny as any other supplier holding your customer information.

Watch for "shadow AI"

This is the term for staff quietly using AI tools you haven't approved or even know about — often with good intentions, just trying to get work done faster. Industry research suggests roughly 1 in 5 organisations experienced a data breach linked to unapproved AI tool use in 2025. The fix isn't banning AI — it's giving staff an approved, easy option so they don't go looking for their own.

83%
of Australians want to be told, and asked for consent, before a business uses AI on their personal information.
ACCC Snapshot on AI Developments, Dec 2025
6
simple practices make up the Australian Government's free national guide to responsible AI adoption — accountability, risk management, human oversight, transparency, privacy, and continuous review.
National AI Centre, "Guidance for AI Adoption," Oct 2025

A simple governance checklist for a small business

  • Write down which AI tools are approved for use — and tell your team plainly what should never be typed into any AI tool (passwords, full customer financial details, health information).
  • Keep a human checking anything AI produces before it reaches a customer or goes into a compliance document.
  • Tell customers, in plain language, where you use AI in your service — it builds trust rather than eroding it.
  • Use the Australian Cyber Security Centre's small business AI guidance and the National AI Centre's "Guidance for AI Adoption" as free, ready-made starting templates — no need to write policy from scratch.

Victoria's own priorities line up with this

In the Victorian Chamber of Commerce and Industry's 2025 Business Pulse survey of over 1,300 businesses, upskilling employees and understanding AI's impact both ranked among the top three enablers of future growth — alongside marketing. Metro businesses rated both slightly higher than regional ones (43% and 39% respectively), suggesting a genuine appetite to learn, not just a fear of missing out.

Three levels of "AI-ready" in a small team

LevelWhat they need
Owner / managerA clear view of where AI could genuinely help the business, and the confidence to say no to tools that don't fit — not deep technical knowledge.
Team leads / supervisorsEnough understanding to know when to trust an AI-produced result and when to double-check it — a "steward," not an expert.
Frontline staffPlain, practical guidance on what they can and can't use AI for day-to-day, and reassurance that flagging an AI mistake is welcomed, not punished.

The one habit that matters most

Reward staff who catch AI mistakes — don't quietly blame them for "not trusting the tool." A team that feels safe pointing out when AI gets something wrong is exactly what keeps an AI tool accurate and useful over time. A team that's afraid to speak up will simply let errors slide through.

What this means for you

  • Have an open conversation with your team about what AI will and won't change for their roles — uncertainty, not the technology itself, is what drives resistance.
  • Set a few simple, written rules rather than relying on "common sense" — everyone interprets that differently.
  • Start with short, practical training tied to real tasks, not a generic one-off webinar. Little and often beats a single big session nobody remembers.
08 · Your Plan

The roadmap: a realistic 100-day plan

Adapted from Mantel's enterprise "brutal audit → hero use case → fund the foundations" approach, scaled down for a small or medium Victorian business.

DAYS 1–30

Step 1 — The honest check-up

Score yourself 0–5

Rate your business honestly on three things: Data (how tidy and centralised is it?), Systems (do your core apps talk to each other?), and People (does your team understand and trust AI?). This tells you which foundation to fix first — before spending a cent on new tools.

DAYS 31–70

Step 2 — One real project, done properly

Not ten pilots — one

Pick a single task that already costs you real time each week and is well-defined enough to hand to AI with confidence. See it through end-to-end: data, tool, training, and a person checking the results — rather than juggling several half-finished trials at once.

DAYS 71–100

Step 3 — Fix the foundations

Invest in what you learned

Use what Step 2 revealed about your weakest link — messy data, disconnected systems, or an anxious team — and put real time or budget into fixing it. This is what makes your next AI project faster and cheaper than the first.

Last word

You don't need to become an AI expert to benefit from this shift — you need to get the boring stuff right: tidy data, connected systems, clear rules, and a team that's been brought along for the journey. Do that, and the "muscle" era of AI becomes a genuine advantage rather than another tool gathering dust.
